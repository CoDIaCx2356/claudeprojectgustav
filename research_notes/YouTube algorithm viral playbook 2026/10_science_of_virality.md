## 1. Psychology of sharing and attention: which levers are supported, and which findings replicated?

### Takeaway
Emotional *intensity* is the most consistently supported content driver of sharing and attention. That means high-arousal emotions such as awe, amusement, excitement, anger and anxiety. Valence is not consistently supported. Positivity predicted e-mail sharing of NYT articles. Negativity and out-group animosity reliably win clicks and social-media shares for news and political content.

Several other levers have good lab or field support:
- curiosity gaps;
- moderate novelty (MAYA, or "optimal differentiation");
- practical value;
- identity and social currency;
- story structure.

Effect sizes are modest, from a few percent to tens of percent per unit, and no single lever guarantees spread.

### Cited Findings

**Emotion and arousal: Berger & Milkman and what followed**
- **Design.** Berger & Milkman studied about 7,000 New York Times articles from roughly three months in 2008 **[verify exact N and dates]**. The outcome was whether an article made the "most e-mailed" list. They controlled for homepage placement and prominence, timing, author and length, and added lab experiments.
- **Results.** Positive content was more viral than negative content. Articles evoking high-arousal emotions (awe, anger, anxiety) were more viral, and articles evoking low-arousal sadness were less viral. Practical value, interest and surprise were also positively associated. — [Berger & Milkman 2012, *Journal of Marketing Research* 49(2):192–205](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.10.0353)
- Reported effect sizes, as change in the odds of making the list for a one-SD increase: anger about +34%, awe about +30%, anxiety about +21%, sadness about −16% **[verify exact %]**. — [Berger & Milkman 2012](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.10.0353)
- **Mechanism test.** Emotional and *physiological* arousal (participants jogged in place) increased willingness to share, even for neutral content. These were small lab studies, and no large preregistered replication was found. — [Berger 2011, *Psychological Science* 22(7):891–893](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797611413294)
- **Video-specific conceptual replication.** In about 800 online videos **[verify N]**, high-arousal emotional videos were shared more than low-arousal ones, and arousal mattered more than valence **[verify]**. — [Nelson-Field, Riebe & Newstead 2013, *Australasian Marketing Journal* 21(4):205–211](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ausmj.2013.07.003)
- **YouTube ads.**
  - An informational focus *reduces* sharing, except for risky products.
  - Positive emotions (amusement, excitement, inspiration, warmth) increase sharing.
  - "Drama" elements (surprise, plot, characters such as babies, animals and celebrities) increase sharing.
  - Prominent early or intermittent branding *reduces* sharing.

  — [Tellis, MacInnis, Tirunillai & Zhang 2019, *Journal of Marketing* 83(4):1–20](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022242919841034)
- Emotional appeals are shared more than informative ones. When the brand is *integral* to the emotional story, the ad gets both the shares and the brand benefit ("valuable virality"). — [Akpinar & Berger 2017, *JMR* 54(2):318–330](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.13.0350)
- **Retention in online video.** An eye-tracking study with automated facial-expression coding of viewers found that eliciting **joy and surprise** concentrated attention and reduced "zapping", i.e. abandoning the video. — [Teixeira, Wedel & Pieters 2012, *JMR* 49(2):144–159](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.10.0207)
- A web-based facial-tracking field study found that entertainment raises viewing interest. Too much entertainment around the brand can lower purchase intent, an entertainment–persuasion trade-off relevant to sponsored segments **[verify details]**. — [Teixeira, Picard & el Kaliouby 2014, *Marketing Science* 33(6):809–827](https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.2014.0854)
- **Attention is fragile at the start.** In about 23 million video views (Akamai CDN data, quasi-experimental design), viewers began abandoning a video once its startup delay passed about 2 seconds. Each further second of delay raised abandonment by about 5.8% **[verify]**. This measures technical delay, not content, but it shows how quickly viewers leave. — [Krishnan & Sitaraman 2012, ACM IMC](https://doi.org/10.1145/2398776.2398799)

**Challenges to "positivity wins": negativity, out-group animosity, moral language**
- **Largest causal test.** Upworthy ran 22,743 randomized headline A/B tests in 2013–2015: about 105,000 headline variants, about 370 million impressions and about 5.7 million clicks. Each extra negative word raised CTR by about 2.3% for an average-length headline, and positive words *decreased* CTR. — [Robertson et al. 2023, *Nature Human Behaviour* 7:812–822](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-023-01538-4)
- **Out-group language.** In about 2.7 million Facebook and Twitter posts from news outlets and US members of Congress, each word referring to the political out-group raised the odds of a share or retweet by about 67%. This was the strongest predictor tested, stronger than negative-emotion or moral-emotional words. — [Rathje, Van Bavel & van der Linden 2021, *PNAS* 118(26):e2024292118](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2024292118)
- **"Moral contagion."** In 563,312 tweets on gun control, same-sex marriage and climate change, each moral-emotional word raised retweets by about 20%. — [Brady et al. 2017, *PNAS* 114(28):7313–7318](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1618923114)
- **Replication flag.** A reanalysis with additional datasets concluded that the moral-contagion effect is *not robust*. Its size and sign varied across datasets and analysis choices. — [Burton, Cruz & Hahn 2021, *Nature Human Behaviour* 5:1629–1635](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-021-01133-5)
- **Negativity bias across cultures.** Physiological reactions (skin conductance, heart rate) to negative video news were stronger than to positive news in a multi-country study (17 countries **[verify]**). — [Soroka, Fournier & Nir 2019, *PNAS* 116(38):18888–18892](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1908369116)

**Curiosity and the information gap**
- **Information-gap theory.** Curiosity is a feeling of deprivation that arises when attention is drawn to a gap between what one knows and what one wants to know.
  - Triggers include questions and puzzles, sequences with an anticipated but unknown resolution, violated expectations, and knowing that someone else holds the information.
  - Curiosity grows as the gap becomes salient and seems closable. People who know a little feel more curiosity than those who know nothing.

  — [Loewenstein 1994, *Psychological Bulletin* 116(1):75–98](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.116.1.75)
- Curiosity follows an **inverted U** with confidence in knowing the answer: it peaks at intermediate uncertainty. Curiosity engaged reward-related regions (caudate) and was associated with better later memory for the answers. — [Kang et al. 2009, *Psychological Science* 20(8):963–973](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2009.02402.x)
- High-curiosity states improved memory for the answer and also for unrelated *incidental* material shown during the curious state (faces). The effect ran through midbrain–hippocampal (dopaminergic) circuits. — [Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath 2014, *Neuron* 84(2):486–496](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2014.08.060)
- Forward-referring headlines ("This is why…", "Here's what happened…") work as a "lure" and are a hallmark of commercial/tabloid clickbait. — [Blom & Hansen 2015, *Journal of Pragmatics* 76:87–100](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pragma.2014.11.010)

**Novelty, surprise, and MAYA ("most advanced yet acceptable")**
- For consumer product designs, typicality and novelty *both* predict aesthetic preference positively, but each suppresses the other. People prefer the design that is "most advanced, yet acceptable". — [Hekkert, Snelders & van Wieringen 2003, *British Journal of Psychology* 94(1):111–124](https://doi.org/10.1348/000712603762842147)
- Classic experimental aesthetics: hedonic value is an inverted-U function of novelty and complexity. — [Berlyne 1970, *Perception & Psychophysics* 8:279–286](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03212593)
- **Optimal differentiation in music.** In about 27,000 Billboard Hot 100 songs (1958–2016), songs moderately differentiated from their contemporaries on audio features charted higher and longer. Very typical and very atypical songs did worse. — [Askin & Mauskapf 2017, *American Sociological Review* 82(5):910–944](https://doi.org/10.1177/0003122417728662)
- Songs whose lyrics were more atypical for their genre were more popular. — [Berger & Packard 2018, *Psychological Science* 29(7):1178–1184](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797618759465)
- **Familiarity.** Repeated exposure increases liking ("mere exposure"). — [Zajonc 1968, *JPSP* 9(2, Pt 2):1–27](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0025848). A meta-analysis found that liking rises and then falls with repetition (inverted U), so familiarity has a ceiling. — [Montoya et al. 2017, *Psychological Bulletin* 143(5):459–498](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000085)
- A popular synthesis presents Loewy's MAYA principle and "familiar surprises" as the formula for hits, drawing on Hekkert's research. — [Thompson 2017, *The Atlantic* (adapted from *Hit Makers*)](https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/2017/01/what-makes-things-cool/508772/)
- **Novelty fades fast.** On Digg, collective attention to new stories decayed as a stretched exponential with a half-life of about an hour (about 69 minutes). — [Wu & Huberman 2007, *PNAS* 104(45):17599–17601](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0704916104)
- **Surprise steers gaze in video.** "Bayesian surprise", i.e. how much each frame changes an observer's expectations, predicted where people looked in video clips better than standard saliency measures. — [Itti & Baldi 2009, *Vision Research* 49(10):1295–1306](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.visres.2008.09.007)

**Narrative transportation (the "Stories" lever)**
- Being "transported" into a narrative increases story-consistent beliefs and reduces counter-arguing. — [Green & Brock 2000, *JPSP* 79(5):701–721](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.79.5.701)
- **Meta-analysis** (about 132 effect sizes from 76 articles **[verify counts]**). Transportation increases story-consistent feelings, beliefs, attitudes and intentions and reduces critical thoughts. Identifiable characters, an imaginable plot and verisimilitude make transportation more likely. — [van Laer, de Ruyter, Visconti & Wetzels 2014, *Journal of Consumer Research* 40(5):797–817](https://doi.org/10.1086/673383)
- **Second meta-analysis.** Narratives have a reliable but small-to-moderate persuasive effect on beliefs, attitudes, intentions and behaviour. — [Braddock & Dillard 2016, *Communication Monographs* 83(4):446–467](https://doi.org/10.1080/03637751.2015.1128555)

**Social currency, identity signalling, triggers, public visibility and practical value (STEPPS)**
- **STEPPS** stands for Social currency, Triggers, Emotion, Public, Practical value and Stories. It is Berger's synthesis of his own and others' research, set out in *Contagious* (2013). — [Jonah Berger (author site)](https://jonahberger.com/)
- **Why people share.** A review identifies five functions of word of mouth: impression management, emotion regulation, information acquisition, social bonding and persuasion. Self-presentation shapes *what* people share. — [Berger 2014, *Journal of Consumer Psychology* 24(4):586–607](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2014.05.002)
- People give up money, about 17–25% of potential earnings, to talk about themselves, and self-disclosure engages the brain's reward system. — [Tamir & Mitchell 2012, *PNAS* 109(21):8038–8043](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1202129109)
- **Self-enhancement.** People *generate* positive word of mouth about their own experiences but *pass on* negative information about other people's experiences. — [De Angelis et al. 2012, *JMR* 49(4):551–563](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.11.0136)
- **Identity signalling.** People diverge from others in identity-relevant domains such as music and clothing. — [Berger & Heath 2007, *JCR* 34(2):121–134](https://doi.org/10.1086/519142). They abandon tastes once dissimilar out-groups adopt them. — [Berger & Heath 2008, *JPSP* 95(3):593–607](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.95.3.593)
- **Controversy.** Moderate controversy *increases* conversation. High controversy *reduces* it because people feel uncomfortable. — [Chen & Berger 2013, *JCR* 40(3):580–593](https://doi.org/10.1086/671465)
- **Triggers and public visibility.** Interesting products got more *immediate* word of mouth but not more *ongoing* word of mouth. Products cued often by the environment, and products that are publicly visible, got more ongoing word of mouth. — [Berger & Schwartz 2011, *JMR* 48(5):869–880](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.48.5.869)
- Environmental cues, such as the colour orange around Halloween, increased evaluations and choice of products linked to the cue. — [Berger & Fitzsimons 2008, *JMR* 45(1):1–14](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.45.1.1)
- **Practical value on YouTube.** In a 2018 survey, about half (51%) of US YouTube users said the site is "very important" for figuring out how to do things they haven't done before **[verify]**. — [Pew Research Center 2018](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2018/11/07/many-turn-to-youtube-for-childrens-content-news-how-to-lessons/)

### Inferences
- **Creator implication: aim for arousal, not positivity.** Design for felt intensity: awe, amusement, excitement, suspense, righteous indignation. The documented sharing penalty is for low-arousal tones such as melancholy or calm (Berger & Milkman; Nelson-Field). Calm content can still win on *watch time*, which is a different outcome from sharing.
- **Creator implication: negativity buys clicks, at a cost.** Negative and out-group framing reliably raises clicks and shares for news and politics (Robertson; Rathje). The per-word effect is small (about 2.3% CTR), and Upworthy and politics may not generalize to entertainment or education. YouTube ranks on watch time and satisfaction (Section 3), so test negative versus neutral framing on *retention and satisfaction*, not CTR alone.
- **Creator implication: keep curiosity gaps moderate and closable.** Curiosity peaks at intermediate uncertainty (Kang 2009). The best title/thumbnail opens a specific, answerable question that the video clearly closes. Vague "you won't believe…" teasers are the clickbait pattern (Blom & Hansen). Viewers remember content better in a curious state (Gruber 2014), which helps recall of the creator's brand.
- **Creator implication: use MAYA as a design rule.** Use a recognizable format or niche convention (familiar) plus one or two novel elements: premise, angle, scale or twist. Too-typical and too-novel content both underperform (Hekkert; Askin & Mauskapf).
- **Creator implication: put surprise inside the video, not only in the packaging.** Surprise and joy keep viewers watching and steer gaze (Teixeira 2012; Itti & Baldi 2009). Schedule "surprise beats" and tension resets, especially where retention usually dips. Front-load value, because viewers abandon within seconds (Krishnan & Sitaraman).
- **Creator implication: use story structure.** Identifiable characters, a goal, stakes and a resolution increase transportation (van Laer 2014). Transportation also lowers counter-arguing, which helps sponsored or persuasive segments.
- **Creator implication: give the sharer status.** People share what makes *them* look smart, funny, in the know or loyal to their group (Berger 2014; Tamir & Mitchell; Berger & Heath). Moderate controversy invites comments; extreme controversy suppresses discussion (Chen & Berger).
- **Creator implication: tie content to triggers.** Link content to frequent everyday cues, recurring calendar moments or recurring formats so it keeps getting re-cued. This drives *ongoing* rather than immediate word of mouth (Berger & Schwartz).
- **Creator implication: practical value is the low-variance route.** Practical value reliably predicts sharing (Berger & Milkman), and how-to content is a core reason people use YouTube (Pew 2018). How-to and explainer content gives steadier, less hit-driven views.
- **Replication scorecard:**
  - *Robust:* arousal → sharing and attention (several conceptual replications); negativity → news clicks (very large set of RCTs); narrative transportation (meta-analyses); curiosity's inverted U (lab); optimal differentiation (large observational datasets).
  - *Contested or context-dependent:* "positive content is more viral" holds for e-mail and brand ads but is contradicted for news clicks and political social shares. "Moral contagion" failed a robustness reanalysis.
  - *Thin:* the physiological-arousal mechanism rests on small lab studies, and STEPPS has never been tested as an integrated model.

### Gaps
- **Watson, van der Linden, Watson & Stillwell (2024), *Scientific Reports*, "Negative online news articles are shared more to social media"** could not be verified. As recalled, it analysed about 95,000 articles from four US/UK news sites against about 579 million Facebook/Twitter posts and found negative articles about 1.9× more likely to be shared to social media. This would be a direct, large-sample challenge to Berger & Milkman's positivity finding. Confirm it before citing.
- **Berger, Moe & Schweidel (2023), *Journal of Marketing*, "What Holds Attention? Linguistic Drivers of Engagement"** could not be verified. It covers which emotional and linguistic features keep people reading and is relevant as a retention analogue, but its samples and coefficients were not confirmed.
- **Quesenberry & Coolsen's work** linking five-act dramatic structure to Super Bowl ad ratings and to YouTube views/shares (2014; a 2019 *Journal of Interactive Marketing* follow-up, "Drama Goes Viral") could not be verified.
- **Replications.** No large preregistered replication of Berger & Milkman's specific emotion coefficients was found.
- **YouTube-native evidence.** Peer-reviewed studies linking measured viewer emotion to *organic* YouTube shares or recommendations, rather than ads, are scarce.
- **STEPPS.** No peer-reviewed test of STEPPS as an integrated predictive model was found.

## 2. Popularity dynamics: heavy tails, cumulative advantage, unpredictability, and how predictable YouTube popularity is

### Takeaway
Popularity on YouTube and similar platforms is extremely heavy-tailed. It is partly self-reinforcing through social proof, rich-get-richer dynamics and broadcast or algorithmic amplification. As a result, identical or similar-quality content can end up with wildly different outcomes. Quality sets the probabilities, not the outcome.

- **Before publishing:** even rich models explain *less than half* of the variance in popularity. What they do explain comes mostly from the creator's existing reach and track record.
- **After publishing:** early trajectories become highly predictive. A few hours of data suffice on fast-decaying platforms. YouTube needed about 10 days in 2008-era data.

### Cited Findings

**Heavy-tailed distributions**
- In 2006–07 crawls of YouTube (and the Korean site Daum), video popularity was Pareto-like. The top 10% of videos accounted for nearly 80% of views, and the popularity curve was a power law with a truncated tail. — [Cha, Kwak, Rodriguez, Ahn & Moon 2007, ACM IMC](https://doi.org/10.1145/1298306.1298309)
- **Daily views of nearly 5 million YouTube videos:**
  - Most activity is accurately described as a Poisson process, i.e. random trickles with no cascade.
  - "Hundreds of thousands" of videos showed bursts followed by power-law relaxation.
  - The relaxation exponents fall into three distinct classes: exogenous vs. endogenous shocks, critical vs. subcritical.
  - This fits an epidemic branching model on a social network with heavy-tailed response times.

  — [Crane & Sornette 2008, *PNAS* 105(41):15649–15653](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0803685105)
- **Methodological caution.** Many claimed "power laws" are better fit statistically by a lognormal or another heavy-tailed form. "Heavy-tailed" is the safer description. — [Clauset, Shalizi & Newman 2009, *SIAM Review* 51(4):661–703](https://doi.org/10.1137/070710111)
- **Mechanisms that generate heavy tails:**
  - cumulative advantage — [Price 1976, *JASIS* 27(5):292–306](https://doi.org/10.1002/asi.4630270505)
  - the Matthew effect — [Merton 1968, *Science* 159(3810):56–63](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.159.3810.56)
  - preferential attachment — [Barabási & Albert 1999, *Science* 286(5439):509–512](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.286.5439.509)

**Experimental evidence on social influence and cumulative advantage**
- **MusicLab.** 14,341 participants could download 48 songs by unknown bands. Eight independent "social-influence worlds" showed download counts, and a control world did not.
  - Social influence increased *both* the inequality and the unpredictability of success.
  - The best songs rarely did poorly and the worst rarely did well, but "any other result was possible".
  - A stronger popularity signal (songs ranked by downloads) amplified both effects.

  — [Salganik, Dodds & Watts 2006, *Science* 311(5762):854–856](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1121066)
- Inverting the displayed popularity created self-fulfilling prophecies: previously unpopular songs became popular. The best songs partly recovered over time. — [Salganik & Watts 2008, *Social Psychology Quarterly* 71(4):338–355](https://doi.org/10.1177/019027250807100404)
- **Herding from a single vote.** On a news aggregator, 101,281 comments were randomly treated. One random up-vote made the first later viewer 32% more likely to up-vote and raised the final mean rating by 25%. Negative manipulations were corrected by the crowd. — [Muchnik, Aral & Taylor 2013, *Science* 341(6146):647–651](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1240466)
- **Field experiments** on Kickstarter, Epinions, Wikipedia and Change.org: randomly assigned early success led to more later success, with diminishing returns to bigger boosts. — [van de Rijt, Kang, Restivo & Patil 2014, *PNAS* 111(19):6934–6939](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1316836111)
- **Beyond music.** In a "parallel worlds" online experiment, which party ended up holding which opinion depended on arbitrary early movers and differed across worlds **[verify design details]**. — [Macy, Deri, Ruch & Tong 2019, *Science Advances* 5(8):eaax0754](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aax0754)
- A popular account of the cumulative-advantage interpretation of hits. — [Watts 2007, *New York Times Magazine*](https://www.nytimes.com/2007/04/15/magazine/15wwlnidealab.t.html)

**Cascade structure: "viral" vs. "broadcast"**
- **Seven online domains.** The vast majority of cascades are small and end within one degree of the seed. Adoptions through long chains of referrals are extremely rare. Even in the largest cascades, most adoptions often happen within one degree of a few dominant individuals. — [Goel, Watts & Goldstein 2012, ACM EC](https://doi.org/10.1145/2229012.2229058)
- **About a billion Twitter diffusion events** (news, videos, images, petitions):
  - Online diffusion shows striking structural diversity. Popular events grow through broadcast, through viral (multi-generation) spread, and through every mix of the two.
  - Even so, "structural virality is typically low, and remains so independent of size". The authors conclude that popularity is largely driven by the size of the largest broadcast.
  - A simple low-infectiousness contagion model on a scale-free network reproduced several findings but not the observed diversity.

  — [Goel, Anderson, Hofman & Watts 2016, *Management Science* 62(1):180–196](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2015.2158)
- **Viral marketing.** Across 16 million product recommendations among 4 million people, most recommendation chains were short and large cascades were rare. — [Leskovec, Adamic & Huberman 2007, *ACM Transactions on the Web* 1(1)](https://doi.org/10.1145/1232722.1232727)
- **Twitter influence** (1.6 million users, 74 million diffusion events, 2009):
  - The largest cascades came from users with many followers and past influence.
  - URLs that workers rated as more interesting or positive spread more.
  - Predicting *which* user or URL would produce a big cascade was unreliable.
  - The authors conclude that word of mouth can be harnessed reliably only by targeting many potential seeds and capturing average effects. "Ordinary influencers" were often the most cost-effective.

  — [Bakshy, Hofman, Mason & Watts 2011, WSDM](https://doi.org/10.1145/1935826.1935845)
- In simulations, large cascades are driven by a critical mass of easily influenced people, not by a few "influentials". — [Watts & Dodds 2007, *JCR* 34(4):441–458](https://doi.org/10.1086/518527)

**Predicting popularity after publishing (early signals)**
- **Early popularity predicts later popularity** on a log scale.
  - On Digg, the first 2 hours predicted 30-day popularity "with remarkable accuracy".
  - YouTube videos had to be tracked for about 10 days to match that accuracy, because they keep being discovered long after upload.
  - Predictions are less accurate for slow-decaying, evergreen content.

  — [Szabo & Huberman 2010, *Communications of the ACM* 53(8):80–88](https://doi.org/10.1145/1787234.1787254)
- Using the *shape* of early daily view counts (multivariate linear and radial-basis-function models) improved YouTube popularity prediction over the single-point Szabo–Huberman model. — [Pinto, Almeida & Gonçalves 2013, WSDM](https://doi.org/10.1145/2433396.2433443)
- **Growth patterns differ by type of video.**
  - Videos later removed for copyright got most of their views early, in an epidemic-like pattern.
  - Top-list videos showed sudden bursts.
  - Randomly sampled videos grew slowly.
  - Search and YouTube-internal mechanisms such as related videos were key referrers.

  — [Figueiredo, Benevenuto & Almeida 2011, WSDM](https://doi.org/10.1145/1935826.1935925)
- **Facebook photo reshares.** Given the first few reshares, the model predicted well whether a cascade would double in size: about 80% accuracy against a 50% baseline **[verify exact figure]**.
  - Temporal features (speed) and structural features were key, and early *breadth* mattered more than depth.
  - Different classes of features performed similarly.
  - **Independent cascades of the same content differed greatly in size**, although the model could still predict which would end up largest.

  — [Cheng, Adamic, Dow, Kleinberg & Leskovec 2014, WWW](https://doi.org/10.1145/2566486.2567997)
- A Hawkes-process model links external promotion (e.g. tweets) to YouTube views. It estimates a video's endogenous "viral potential", which allows forecasts of how much a video will respond to promotion. — [Rizoiu, Xie, Sanner, Cebrian, Yu & Van Hentenryck 2017, WWW](https://doi.org/10.1145/3038912.3052650)

**Predicting popularity before publishing (content and creator features)**
- **Twitter cascade size.** Even with very rich data on users, content and past performance, the best models explained *less than half* of the variance. The user's past success was the strongest feature. Simulations showed that even slight uncertainty about quality tightly bounds how predictable success can be before posting. — [Martin, Hofman, Sharma, Anderson & Watts 2016, WWW](https://doi.org/10.1145/2872427.2883001). For the framing, see [Hofman, Sharma & Watts 2017, *Science* 355(6324):486–488](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aal3856)
- **YouTube "clones".** Studying the same content uploaded several times holds the content fixed.
  - Popularity showed a strong linear rich-get-richer pattern.
  - The number of previous views was the most important factor, except for very young videos. Video age came second.
  - First-mover advantage and search bias toward already-popular videos both contributed.

  — [Borghol, Ardon, Carlsson, Eager & Mahanti 2012, KDD](https://doi.org/10.1145/2339530.2339717)
- **Flickr images.** In about 2.3 million images, social-context features (followers, groups) predicted popularity much better than image-content features. Rank correlation was about 0.8 for social context versus about 0.3–0.4 for content alone **[verify exact values]**. — [Khosla, Das Sarma & Hamid 2014, WWW](https://doi.org/10.1145/2566486.2567996)
- **Wording, with author and content held fixed.** Using pairs of tweets by the same author linking the same URL, a classifier picked the better-spreading wording about 65% of the time. Humans managed about 61%, against a 50% baseline **[verify exact]**. — [Tan, Lee & Pang 2014, ACL](https://aclanthology.org/P14-1017/)
- **Reddit resubmissions.** The same image was submitted many times, and its success varied enormously with the title, the community and the timing of each submission. — [Lakkaraju, McAuley & Leskovec 2013, ICWSM](https://cs.stanford.edu/people/jure/pubs/reddit-icwsm13.pdf)
- In a model, heavy-tailed meme popularity can emerge from limited attention plus network structure, even without intrinsic quality differences. — [Weng, Flammini, Vespignani & Menczer 2012, *Scientific Reports* 2:335](https://doi.org/10.1038/srep00335)
- **Retraction flag.** A high-profile paper claimed that, under limited attention, low- and high-quality information are about equally likely to go viral. The authors retracted it in 2019 after finding errors, so the strong "quality doesn't matter" claim is unsupported. — [Qiu et al. 2017, *Nature Human Behaviour* 1:0132 (retracted)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-017-0132)

### Inferences
- **How much is predictable before vs. after publishing:**
  - *Before:* at best under half the variance (Martin 2016), and content-only features add modest signal (Khosla; Tan).
  - Most of the pre-publication signal is the creator's existing reach and track record (Martin; Bakshy; Borghol).
  - *After:* early trajectories become highly predictive. That means hours on fast-decay feeds, about 10 days on 2008-era YouTube (Szabo & Huberman), and about 80% accuracy on "will it double?" after a few shares (Cheng).
  - No verified source gives a modern, YouTube-specific split (see Gaps).
- **Creator implication: treat each upload as one draw from a heavy-tailed distribution.** A single video's result is weak evidence about skill. Judge strategy on rolling portfolios of 10–20+ uploads, not on single hits or flops. Identical content can get very different outcomes (Cheng; MusicLab; Borghol).
- **Creator implication: watch early signals, then intervene.** Early performance is the best predictor of final performance. Set decision checkpoints: hours to days for videos fed by browse/home traffic, longer for evergreen search content (Szabo & Huberman found evergreen content less predictable early). If early click-through or retention lags the channel's baseline, re-package the title and thumbnail while the system is still testing the video.
- **Creator implication: on YouTube the recommender is the dominant "broadcaster".** Most spread is shallow and hub-driven, and popularity tracks the largest broadcast (Goel 2012/2016; Leskovec 2007). Design for the signals the recommender uses (Section 3) rather than relying on long person-to-person share chains. Shareability still adds extra generations of spread.
- **Creator implication: use rich-get-richer deliberately.** Prior views and social proof attract further views (Borghol; Muchnik; van de Rijt). Route traffic from hits to new uploads with end screens, playlists, sequels, pinned comments and community posts. Make videos that are natural "next watches" after popular videos in your niche (Zhou 2010, Section 3).
- **Creator implication: seed widely rather than hoping for a hit.** Diffusion can be harnessed reliably only through many seeds capturing average effects (Bakshy 2011). Collaborations, cross-posting and community distribution all add seeds.
- **What "viral on command" can and cannot mean:**
  - *Cannot:* guarantee that any single video goes viral. Pre-publication predictability is bounded well below certainty.
  - *Can:* raise the expected value and fatten the right tail of each upload through quality, packaging and audience; detect winners faster from early signals; take more draws through volume; and amplify early winners through broadcast and recommendation.
- **Quality still matters.** In MusicLab the best songs rarely flopped, and the "quality doesn't matter" virality paper was retracted. Skill shifts the probabilities; luck picks the particular outcome.

### Gaps
- **No modern YouTube-specific split.** No peer-reviewed study verified here gives a 2020s, YouTube-specific breakdown of variance explained before vs. after publishing, or any Shorts-specific analysis. The canonical prediction papers use 2006–2014 data, from before major recommender changes.
- **Deep-learning thumbnail and title models.** These exist in the CS literature, for example video-popularity regression papers such as Trzciński & Rokita 2017 (*IEEE Transactions on Multimedia*) and multimodal "social media popularity prediction" challenge entries. I could not verify their accuracy figures, or whether they exclude creator and subscriber features. The content-only share of predictability on YouTube is therefore unquantified.
- **Figures to check against the papers:**
  - Cheng et al. (2014) accuracy figure.
  - Khosla et al. (2014) correlation values.
  - Goel et al. (2016): one memory is that a pre-publication (working-paper) version stressed that the very largest events showed *high* structural virality. The published abstract (cited above) concludes popularity is largely driven by the largest broadcast. Cite the published version.

## 3. Audits of YouTube's recommender: what does it favour?

### Takeaway
YouTube's own engineering papers and independent audits agree that the recommender:
- optimizes predicted **watch time plus satisfaction**, not raw clicks, and YouTube names clickbait as the reason;
- deliberately models **freshness** (an "example age" feature);
- tends to route viewers toward **already-popular and longer** videos;
- is **heavily personalized** by watch history;
- drives most viewing (about 70% of watch time per YouTube in 2018).

Political audits from 2019–2025 mostly find that it reflects users' own preferences and tends to moderate rather than radicalize. Crowdsourced audits show that it still surfaces content users later regret, and that viewers' negative-feedback buttons are weak.

### Cited Findings

**YouTube's published design**
- **Deep-learning recommender (2016):**
  - It has two stages: candidate generation, then ranking.
  - Inputs include watch history, search history and demographics.
  - An "example age" feature was added because "users prefer fresh content".
  - Ranking optimizes *expected watch time*, because ranking by click-through rate "often promotes deceptive videos that the user does not complete ('clickbait')".

  — [Covington, Adams & Sargin 2016, ACM RecSys](https://doi.org/10.1145/2959100.2959190)
- **Later multitask ranker (2019).** It optimizes engagement objectives (e.g. clicks, watch time) *and* satisfaction objectives (e.g. likes, dismissals), using a mixture-of-experts model. A "shallow tower" corrects for position and selection bias. — [Zhao et al. 2019, ACM RecSys](https://doi.org/10.1145/3298689.3346997)
- **YouTube's public account (2021).**
  - Signals include clicks, watch time, survey responses, shares, likes and dislikes.
  - YouTube shifted emphasis from clicks/views toward watch time in 2012.
  - It uses user surveys to weight "valued watchtime".
  - YouTube says recommendations drive more viewing than subscriptions or search.

  — [YouTube Official Blog (Goodrow) 2021](https://blog.youtube/inside-youtube/on-youtubes-recommendation-system/)
- YouTube's Chief Product Officer said more than 70% of time spent watching YouTube is driven by recommendations (CES, January 2018). — [CNET 2018](https://www.cnet.com/tech/services-and-software/youtube-ces-2018-neal-mohan/)

**What audits found it favours: popularity, length, recency, channel type**
- **Popularity and length (Pew, 2018).** Pew followed random chains of recommendations from a logged-out, history-free browser in mid-2018. Recommendations led to progressively *longer* and *more popular* videos, and a large share of recommendations went to videos with millions of views. **[Verify exact figures: average length by step, share of recommendations to videos with more than 1 million views.]** — [Pew Research Center 2018](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2018/11/07/many-turn-to-youtube-for-childrens-content-news-how-to-lessons/)
- **Neighbour popularity and position (2010 data).**
  - Related-video recommendations were the main view source for the *majority* of videos, while search was the largest source overall.
  - A video's views correlated strongly with the views of the videos that referred traffic to it.
  - A video's position in the related list strongly affected click-through.
  - Overall, the recommender *increased* the diversity of what was watched.

  — [Zhou, Khemmarat & Gao 2010, ACM IMC](https://doi.org/10.1145/1879141.1879193)
- **Search bias toward the already popular** feeds rich-get-richer dynamics. — [Borghol et al. 2012, KDD](https://doi.org/10.1145/2339530.2339717)
- **Mainstream channels.** A logged-out audit (2019) reported that recommendations favoured mainstream-media channels over independent channels. Critics note it had no personalization. **[verify DOI/details]** — [Ledwich & Zaitsev 2020, *First Monday* 25(3)](https://doi.org/10.5210/fm.v25i3.10419)
- **Search "ranking cultures."** An audit of YouTube search results over time found that YouTube-native channels figured prominently. Rankings were fairly stable for evergreen queries and volatile for news-driven ones **[verify details]**. — [Rieder, Matamoros-Fernández & Coromina 2018, *Convergence* 24(1):50–68](https://doi.org/10.1177/1354856517736982)

**Personalization, radicalization and moderation audits (2019–2025)**
- **Radicalization pathways.** The audit covered about 331,000 videos from 349 channels, 72 million+ comments and about 2 million recommendations. Commenting users migrated over time from milder to more extreme political channels, and recommendations could lead from milder to extreme channels. The recommendations were collected without personalization. — [Ribeiro, Ottoni, West, Almeida & Meira 2020, ACM FAT*](https://doi.org/10.1145/3351095.3372879)
- **Nationally representative panel** (300,000+ US panelists **[verify]**, 2016–2019). Consumption of far-right content reflected users' preferences and wider web habits. There was little evidence that the recommender drove it. — [Hosseinmardi et al. 2021, *PNAS* 118(32):e2101967118](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2101967118)
- **Browser data from 1,181 participants (2020).** Exposure to alternative and extremist channels was concentrated among a small group with high prior resentment, usually channel *subscribers*. Recommendations to such videos were rare for non-subscribers. — [Chen, Nyhan, Reifler, Robertson & Wilson 2023, *Science Advances* 9(35):eadd8080](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.add8080)
- **"Counterfactual bots."** Bots replayed real users' watch histories, then either followed the user's actual choices or followed only the recommender. Following the recommender alone produced *less* partisan consumption. The sidebar "forgets" a partisan history after roughly 30 videos **[verify]**. — [Hosseinmardi et al. 2024, *PNAS* 121(8):e2313377121](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2313377121)
- **Sock-puppet audit** (about 100,000 puppets **[verify]**). Recommendations were ideologically congenial, and more so deeper into a trail, especially for right-leaning puppets. A small but rising share came from problematic channels. — [Haroon et al. 2023, *PNAS* 120(50):e2213020120](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2213020120)
- **Naturalistic experiments** with a YouTube-like interface whose recommendations were manipulated. Short-term exposure to filter-bubble recommendations had limited effects on polarization **[verify N ≈ 9,000 and DOI]**. — [Liu et al. 2025, *PNAS*](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2318127122)
- **Systematic review of 23 studies.** Most implicated the recommender in pathways to problematic content; some were mixed and few found none **[verify split, recalled as 14/7/2]**. — [Yesilada & Lewandowsky 2022, *Internet Policy Review* 11(1)](https://doi.org/10.14763/2022.1.1652)

**Crowdsourced audits (Mozilla Foundation)**
- ***YouTube Regrets* (2021).** 37,380 browser-extension volunteers filed 3,362 reports between July 2020 and May 2021 **[verify all figures]**.
  - 71% of the videos people regretted watching had been *recommended*.
  - Recommended videos were 40% more likely to be regretted than searched-for ones.
  - Regret rates were 60% higher in countries where English is not the main language.

  — [Mozilla Foundation 2021](https://foundation.mozilla.org/en/youtube/findings/)
- ***Does This Button Work?* (2022).** The study covered 22,722 participants and about 567 million recommended videos **[verify]**. Share of unwanted recommendations each control prevented:
  - "Don't recommend channel": about 43%
  - "Remove from watch history": about 29%
  - "Dislike": about 12%
  - "Not interested": about 11%

  — [Mozilla Foundation, YouTube research hub](https://foundation.mozilla.org/en/youtube/)

### Inferences
- **Creator implication: optimize the click *and* what follows it.** The ranking objective explicitly discounts clicks that don't turn into watch time and satisfaction (Covington; Zhao; Goodrow). Packaging that wins clicks but loses viewers early is penalized by design.
- **Creator implication: freshness is built in.** The "example age" feature means new uploads get a window of recency-boosted exposure. Publishing consistently keeps a channel in the candidate pools. How large this effect is for creators has never been measured causally.
- **Creator implication: popularity and length bias cut both ways.** Recommendations drifted toward longer, more popular videos (Pew 2018), and a video's views tracked the popularity of the videos that referred traffic to it (Zhou 2010). Make content that fits as the "next video" after popular videos in your topic cluster: similar topic, format and audience. Length helps only if retention holds, because the objective is expected watch time and satisfaction, not raw duration.
- **Creator implication: personalization rewards a coherent audience.** Recommendations are driven by each viewer's history, and the recommender "forgets" quickly (Hosseinmardi 2024). A consistent topic and audience profile plausibly helps the system find look-alike viewers. Abrupt pivots plausibly dilute that signal (inference).
- **Creator implication: negative feedback is asymmetric.** Viewer controls are weak, except "Don't recommend channel" (Mozilla 2022). So one antagonizing upload may lose an individual viewer at the channel level. This is an inference from viewer-side data, not a measured effect on creators.
- **Generalizability caveat.** Most independent audits are about political or problematic content, often in logged-out sessions. Evidence on how the recommender treats entertainment or education creators of different sizes is thin.

### Gaps
- **No audits found on:**
  - **YouTube Shorts** recommendations;
  - **small vs. large channels** with content held constant (Borghol 2012 comes closest and is old).
- **Unconfirmed figures.** Pew's exact random-walk figures (2018), Mozilla's percentages and several audit sample sizes are marked [verify]. They could not be rechecked because the hosts were blocked.
- **YouTube's newer tooling** was not verified and should come from YouTube Help/official sources (for the platform-mechanics researcher):
  - Thumbnail/title "Test & Compare", which as recalled picks winners by watch-time share rather than CTR.
  - How satisfaction surveys are used today.

## 4. Distribution facts: how concentrated are YouTube views, and how often do small channels break out?

### Takeaway
YouTube attention is extremely concentrated:
- the top 10% of videos got about 80% of views in 2006–07 crawls (Cha et al.);
- about 3% of channels got about 85% of views (Bärtl 2018 **[verify]**);
- even among channels with 250k+ subscribers, about 10% of videos took about 79% of first-week views (Pew 2019 **[verify]**).

Random sampling of the whole platform shows the typical (median) video gets very few views. There is no rigorous, peer-reviewed statistic on how often small channels break out.

### Cited Findings
- The top 10% of videos accounted for nearly 80% of views. Popularity followed a power law with a truncated tail (YouTube and Daum, 2006–07). — [Cha et al. 2007, ACM IMC](https://doi.org/10.1145/1298306.1298309)
- In a statistical analysis of YouTube channels over 2006–2016, the roughly 3% most-viewed channels attracted roughly 85% of all views. Most channels upload rarely and attract small audiences **[verify sample size and exact %]**. — [Bärtl 2018, *Convergence* 24(1):16–32](https://doi.org/10.1177/1354856517736979)
- ***A Week in the Life of Popular YouTube Channels* (Pew, 2019)** **[verify all figures]**:
  - About 43,770 channels with at least 250,000 subscribers posted about 243,000 videos in the first week of 2019.
  - The 10% most-viewed of those videos took about 79% of their first-week views.
  - Videos featuring children under 13 averaged nearly three times the views of other videos.

  — [Pew Research Center 2019](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2019/07/25/a-week-in-the-life-of-popular-youtube-channels/)
- **Random sample of YouTube.** Researchers built a random sample by "dialing" random video IDs. Numerically, the platform is dominated by low-view, personal and amateur uploads, and the typical video's view count is in the tens, not thousands **[the specific median, total-video estimate and concentration figures need verification]**. — [McGrady, Zheng, Curran, Baumgartner & Zuckerman 2023, "Dialing for Videos: A Random Sample of YouTube", *Journal of Quantitative Description: Digital Media*](https://journalqd.org/)
- Across nearly 5 million videos, most daily view series looked like random (Poisson) noise with no cascade. Only a minority, though numbering in the hundreds of thousands, showed burst dynamics. — [Crane & Sornette 2008, *PNAS*](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0803685105)
- Randomly sampled YouTube videos grew slowly, unlike top-list videos, which showed sudden bursts. — [Figueiredo, Benevenuto & Almeida 2011, WSDM](https://doi.org/10.1145/1935826.1935925)
- In 2010, related-video recommendations were the main view source for most videos, and the recommender increased the overall diversity of viewing. This is the mechanism by which long-tail videos get found. — [Zhou, Khemmarat & Gao 2010, ACM IMC](https://doi.org/10.1145/1879141.1879193)
- **Music-streaming analogue (2023):** of about 184 million tracks on audio streaming, the large majority got 1,000 or fewer plays, and about 45.6 million got zero plays **[verify figures and URL]**. — [Luminate 2023 Year-End Music Report](https://luminatedata.com/reports/yearend-music-industry-report-2023/)

### Inferences
- **Creator implication: know the base rates.** The *median* outcome of an upload is tiny, and the *mean* is driven by rare outliers. Plan finances and morale around the median, and plan growth strategy around raising the odds of an outlier.
- **Concentration feeds itself** through rich-get-richer dynamics and the recommender's popularity bias (Sections 2–3). Early traction compounds, so a creator's first audience and first hits matter disproportionately.
- **Breakouts happen through the recommender.** It is the main discovery path for long-tail videos and increases the overall diversity of viewing (Zhou 2010). This is the evidence-based reason small channels *can* break out: the system shows videos to people who never subscribed. How often it happens is not quantified in peer-reviewed work.
- **Use relative benchmarks.** Compare each video with the channel's own trailing baseline. Comparisons across channels are confounded by audience size (Borghol; Martin).

### Gaps
- **Breakout frequency.** No peer-reviewed estimate was found of how often small channels (say, under 1,000 subscribers) produce a video that reaches a high view threshold, or how Shorts changed this. This is a key open question for the playbook.
- **"Dialing for Videos" numbers need checking.** As recalled from the paper and later coverage by its authors:
  - total videos on the order of 10+ billion;
  - a median view count in the low double digits;
  - a non-trivial share of videos with zero views;
  - videos with more than 10,000 views (a few percent of all videos) capturing the large majority of total views.

  The journal page could not be opened.
- **Post-2020 concentration data.** No verified peer-reviewed source gives figures such as the share of views going to the top 1% of channels, including Shorts. YouTube does not publish distribution statistics.

## 5. Clickbait, thumbnails (faces and emotions), and title sentiment

### Takeaway
Packaging measurably shifts clicks:
- Each negative word raised CTR by about 2.3% across 22,743 headline A/B tests.
- On Instagram, photos with faces were 38% more likely to get likes.
- Faces draw the eyes within about 100 ms, and text also attracts gaze.

YouTube, however, ranks on watch time and satisfaction rather than clicks, explicitly to defeat clickbait. Experimental evidence that clickbait harms trust is mixed: small or null effects in news studies. Satisfaction theory predicts that over-promising backfires. No rigorous, peer-reviewed causal study of YouTube thumbnails' effect on CTR could be verified.

### Cited Findings

**Definitions and detection**
- **Academic definition:** clickbait consists of teaser messages that withhold information in order to entice readers to click through. — [Potthast, Köpsel, Stein & Hagen 2016, ECIR](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-30671-1_72)
- Clickbait headlines can be detected reliably from linguistic markers such as forward reference, hyperbole and second-person address. The authors built a browser extension that flags them. — [Chakraborty, Paranjape, Kakarla & Ganguly 2016, IEEE/ACM ASONAM](https://doi.org/10.1109/ASONAM.2016.7752207)
- **Forward reference as a lure.** — [Blom & Hansen 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pragma.2014.11.010)
- **YouTube-specific detection.** Clickbait videos can be detected from title, thumbnail, tags and comments. In 2018, YouTube's recommendations did not appear to filter clickbait out **[verify finding and DOI]**. — [Zannettou, Chatzis, Papadamou & Sirivianos 2018, IEEE S&P Workshops](https://doi.org/10.1109/SPW.2018.00018)

**Platform stance, and effects on trust and satisfaction**
- YouTube moved its ranking to expected watch time because CTR-based ranking promoted "deceptive videos that the user does not complete". — [Covington et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1145/2959100.2959190)
- YouTube says it shifted from clicks toward watch time in 2012 and added survey-based "valued watchtime" and satisfaction signals. — [YouTube Official Blog 2021](https://blog.youtube/inside-youtube/on-youtubes-recommendation-system/)
- Survey experiments found clickbait headlines had essentially *null* effects on polarization, trust in media and learning. — [Munger, Luca, Nagler & Tucker 2020, *Public Opinion Quarterly* 84(1):49–73](https://doi.org/10.1093/poq/nfaa008)
- An experiment found clickbait-style headlines slightly lowered perceived journalistic quality and credibility **[verify magnitude and DOI]**. — [Molyneux & Coddington 2020, *Journalism Practice* 14(4):429–446](https://doi.org/10.1080/17512786.2019.1628658)
- **Expectation disconfirmation:** satisfaction depends on performance *relative to expectations*. Raising expectations without delivering lowers satisfaction. — [Oliver 1980, *JMR* 17(4):460–469](https://doi.org/10.2307/3150499)

**Title and headline sentiment**
- Across 22,743 headline A/B tests (Upworthy, 2013–2015), each negative word raised CTR by about 2.3%, and positive words lowered it. Upworthy's audience and "uplifting" brand limit how far this generalizes. — [Robertson et al. 2023, *Nature Human Behaviour*](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-023-01538-4)
- The open dataset behind this contains 32,487 Upworthy headline/packaging experiments and is usable for packaging research **[verify count]**. — [Matias, Munger, Le Quere & Ebersole 2021, *Scientific Data* 8:195](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41597-021-00934-7)
- In political posts, each word referring to the out-group raised the odds of sharing by about 67%. — [Rathje et al. 2021, *PNAS*](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2024292118)
- **Wording alone changes spread.** It matters on Twitter with author and URL held fixed — [Tan, Lee & Pang 2014](https://aclanthology.org/P14-1017/). Title choice also shaped the success of the identical image on Reddit — [Lakkaraju et al. 2013](https://cs.stanford.edu/people/jure/pubs/reddit-icwsm13.pdf)

**Faces, emotion and gaze (relevant to thumbnails)**
- In 1.1 million Instagram photos, photos with faces were 38% more likely to receive likes and 32% more likely to receive comments. The number, age and gender of the faces made no significant difference. — [Bakhshi, Shamma & Gilbert 2014, ACM CHI](https://doi.org/10.1145/2556288.2557403)
- **Eye tracking:** faces *and text* attract gaze quickly and largely regardless of the viewer's task. — [Cerf, Frady & Koch 2009, *Journal of Vision* 9(12):10](https://doi.org/10.1167/9.12.10)
- **Ultra-rapid face detection:** people's eyes can start moving toward a face within about 100 ms. — [Crouzet, Kirchner & Thorpe 2010, *Journal of Vision* 10(4):16](https://doi.org/10.1167/10.4.16)
- **Contested assumption:** once confounds were controlled, *happy* faces, not angry ones, were detected more efficiently in a crowd, contrary to the "anger superiority" hypothesis. — [Becker, Anderson, Mortensen, Neufeld & Neel 2011, *JEP: General* 140(4):637–659](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0024060)
- Eliciting joy and surprise kept viewers watching online video ads. — [Teixeira, Wedel & Pieters 2012](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.10.0207)
- Curiosity peaks at intermediate uncertainty (an inverted U). This argues for specific, partly revealing packaging rather than total mystery. — [Kang et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2009.02402.x)

### Inferences
- **Creator implication: use a clear, expressive face when the format allows.** Faces capture gaze within about 100 ms and raise engagement on image feeds (Bakhshi; Crouzet; Cerf). The evidence does *not* show that exaggerated or negative "shock faces" beat genuine positive or surprised expressions. Happy faces are detected more efficiently (Becker 2011), so test the expression rather than assuming it.
- **Creator implication: thumbnail text gets read.** Text attracts gaze much as faces do (Cerf 2009). Keep it to a few words, and make it complement the title rather than repeat it.
- **Creator implication: use negativity sparingly and honestly.** Negative words lift clicks by low single-digit percentages per word (Robertson). On YouTube the payoff depends on whether the video delivers on the framing, because ranking weighs watch time and satisfaction.
- **Creator implication: the honest curiosity gap is the evidence-backed sweet spot.** Make the promise specific (moderate uncertainty, per Kang), then deliver early and fully, since unmet expectations lower satisfaction (Oliver). Over-promising clickbait works against the ranking objective (Covington), even though trust effects in news studies are small (Munger).
- **Creator implication: test packaging systematically.** Wording changes outcomes even with content held fixed (Tan; Lakkaraju), and humans are only modestly better than chance at picking the winner (Tan). Use the platform's A/B tools where available and judge by watch-time-weighted results.

### Gaps
- **No YouTube thumbnail study verified.** I could verify no peer-reviewed causal study of how YouTube thumbnails (faces, emotion, text, colour) affect YouTube CTR or views. Academic studies fall back on views as a confounded proxy, because impression and CTR data are private. A candidate study on thumbnail visual attributes for branded YouTube videos (*Decision Support Systems*, around 2022) could not be checked.
- **No YouTube title-sentiment study verified.** No peer-reviewed study of title sentiment vs. views on YouTube that controls for channel size was verified. Robertson 2023 is about news headlines on Upworthy.
- **Platform policy not verified:** YouTube's 2024 enforcement against "egregious clickbait" (starting in India) and the mechanics of Test & Compare.
- **Pew 2020:** a Pew analysis of popular YouTube news channels, recalled as finding independent channels more negative in tone than established news outlets, was not verified.

## 6. Repeatability: shots on goal, hit rates, iteration and testing

### Takeaway
Across science, film, music and art, a creator's biggest hit behaves like a random draw from that creator's own quality distribution:
- the timing of the best work is random (the "random-impact rule");
- the hit rate per attempt stays roughly constant (Simonton's "equal-odds rule");
- success breaks down into a stable personal skill parameter, Q, multiplied by luck.

The controllable levers are:
- raising Q through craft and packaging;
- taking more, cheaper shots;
- exploring before exploiting;
- running many small experiments (most fail, and the gains come from rare big wins);
- getting peers, rather than yourself or managers, to judge ideas.

### Cited Findings
- **Equal-odds rule.** Across creative careers, the ratio of hits to total works stays roughly constant. The most prolific creators produce the most hits *and* the most flops, so quantity predicts the number of hits. — [Simonton 1997, *Psychological Review* 104(1):66–89](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.104.1.66)
- **Random-impact rule and the Q-model.** A scientist's most-cited paper is equally likely to be any paper in their sequence. Impact is modelled as a stable individual parameter Q multiplied by a random luck factor p, and Q predicts future impact. — [Sinatra, Wang, Deville, Song & Barabási 2016, *Science* 354(6312):aaf5239](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aaf5239)
- The same random-impact pattern and Q × luck decomposition appear in creative careers, e.g. film directors and music artists **[verify exact domains]**. — [Janosov, Battiston & Sinatra 2020, *EPJ Data Science* 9:9](https://doi.org/10.1140/epjds/s13688-020-00227-w)
- **Hot streaks.** Most artists, film directors and scientists have at least one hot streak (about 91%, 82% and 90% respectively **[verify]**), and usually only one. Its timing within a career is random, and people are not more productive during it. — [Liu et al. 2018, *Nature* 559:396–399](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-018-0315-8)
- Hot streaks tend to begin after a period of *exploration* (diverse work) followed by *exploitation* (focused work) **[verify citation details]**. — [Liu et al. 2021, *Nature Communications* 12:5392](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-021-25477-8)
- **Art careers.** Exhibition histories of about half a million artists show that early access to prestigious venues locks in long-term success. Artists who start at the periphery mostly drop out. Network position compounds. — [Fraiberger, Sinatra, Resch, Riedl & Barabási 2018, *Science* 362(6416):825–829](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aau7224)
- **Film.** Box-office revenues for about 2,000 films released in 1984–1996 **[verify N]** are so heavy-tailed that their variance is effectively infinite. Stars raise expected revenue but do not make outcomes predictable ("nobody knows"). — [De Vany & Walls 1999, *Journal of Cultural Economics* 23(4):285–318](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1007608125988)
- **Superstar economics.** Small differences in talent become huge differences in reward when one performer can serve a mass audience at low marginal cost. — [Rosen 1981, *American Economic Review* 71(5):845–858](https://www.jstor.org/stable/1803469)
- **Model, not data.** An agent-based "talent vs. luck" simulation shows that the most successful agents are rarely the most talented. — [Pluchino, Biondo & Rapisarda 2018, *Advances in Complex Systems* 21(3–4):1850014](https://doi.org/10.1142/S0219525918500145)
- **Learning from failure.** Across NIH grant applications, startups and security datasets, those who eventually succeeded improved systematically between attempts, reusing and refining parts of earlier ones. Those who never succeeded did not. — [Yin, Wang, Evans & Wang 2019, *Nature* 575:190–194](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-019-1725-y)
- **Who should judge ideas.** In circus-arts data, creators forecasting *peers'* novel ideas were more accurate than managers, who undervalued novelty. Creators overrated their *own* ideas. — [Berg 2016, *Administrative Science Quarterly* 61(3):433–468](https://doi.org/10.1177/0001839216642211)
- **Testing hit rates.** Only about 10–20% of online experiments at Google and Bing produce positive results. Across Microsoft, about a third are positive, a third neutral and a third negative. One small change to how Bing displayed ad headlines raised revenue by about 12%. — [Kohavi & Thomke 2017, *Harvard Business Review*](https://hbr.org/2017/09/the-surprising-power-of-online-experiments)
- **Fat tails favour many small tests.** In Bing's experiment data, treatment effects are fat-tailed. The best strategy is therefore "lean experimentation": many small, cheap tests to find rare big winners. Thin tails would favour fewer, larger tests. — [Azevedo, Deng, Montiel Olea, Rao & Weyl 2020, *Journal of Political Economy* 128(12):4614–4672](https://doi.org/10.1086/710607)
- **Many seeds beat picking winners.** Diffusion can be harnessed reliably only by targeting many potential seeds and capturing average effects. — [Bakshy et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1145/1935826.1935845)
- **Quality bounds luck.** In MusicLab the best songs rarely did poorly and the worst rarely did well, even though outcomes in between were highly unpredictable. — [Salganik et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1121066)

### Inferences
- **Creator implication: volume, with quality control.** Under the equal-odds and random-impact rules, the number of *good-faith, well-made* attempts is the main controllable driver of how many hits a creator gets.
  - Illustrative arithmetic, not a cited finding: if each upload has a 5% chance of becoming an outlier for the channel, 20 uploads give about a 64% chance of at least one outlier (1 − 0.95^20), and 50 uploads give about 92%.
  - Raising the per-upload chance (Q) shifts the whole curve.
- **Creator implication: raise Q deliberately.** Q is stable, but not fixed. Better packaging, retention craft and topic selection raise the odds of every attempt. Learning goes fastest when iteration is quick and deliberate, with each attempt reusing and refining the last (Yin 2019).
- **Creator implication: explore, then exploit.** Hot streaks follow exploration (Liu 2021). Spend a fixed share of uploads on new formats or topics. Double down hard when one shows above-baseline early signals (Szabo & Huberman; Cheng).
- **Creator implication: test like a lab, not like an artist.** Most experiments fail (Kohavi), and the gains come from rare big winners (Azevedo). Run many cheap packaging and format tests, drop losers quickly and scale winners. Don't over-trust your own taste: creators overrate their own ideas and peers forecast better (Berg 2016). Set up peer feedback groups of other creators.
- **Creator implication: network position compounds** (Fraiberger 2018; Borghol 2012). Early collaborations with established channels, and being placed in popular "next watch" slots, are the YouTube equivalent of early access to prestigious galleries.
- **What "going viral on command" can and cannot mean:**
  - *Cannot:* guarantee that a specific video becomes a hit. Even rich pre-publication models explain under half the variance (Martin 2016), and identical content can meet very different fates (Cheng 2014; MusicLab; Borghol's clones).
  - *Can:* reliably raise each upload's expected value and its chance of an outlier, detect winners early and amplify them, and run a portfolio in which hits are close to inevitable overall even though each one stays individually unpredictable.
  - Mega-creators look "repeatable" largely because they combine high Q, a huge broadcast base (subscribers plus the recommender's trust) and high volume. Survivorship bias hides the many equally skilled creators who lacked that broadcast base.

### Gaps
- **Upload frequency.** No peer-reviewed causal estimate was found of how upload frequency affects YouTube channel growth or views per video, including possible cannibalization between uploads.
- **YouTube hit rates.** No peer-reviewed hit-rate statistics for YouTube creators were found (e.g. the share of a channel's videos exceeding 10× its median). Practitioner data exists but was out of scope and unverified.
- **Hoiles, Aprem & Krishnamurthy (2017), *IEEE Transactions on Knowledge and Data Engineering*, "Engagement and Popularity Dynamics of YouTube Videos and Sensitivity to Meta-Data"** could not be verified. As recalled, it analysed about 6 million videos from a multi-channel network, including the effect of changes to title, thumbnail and keywords. If confirmed, it would give YouTube-specific evidence on metadata iteration.
- **Music and publishing hit rates.** Label "hit-rate" statistics for music, and publishing statistics such as BookScan-based claims about the share of books selling under X copies, are often mis-cited online and were not verified, so they are excluded.
